On Tuesday 10 November we ran the third and last evening of Economic Cultures at Design Academy Eindhoven, with Gemma Medina and Ned Kaar, DAE Alumni Coordinator. Two modules: Interdependence, where teams budgeted a project together, then, after the break, the second part of Seeds of Hope around an editor's table. This is a brief recollection of that evening, and of the three evenings as a whole.

## A tighter group

We worked with a smaller group than on the first two evenings. That was to be expected. DAE is a very international school, and once the studies and the graduation show are done, many graduates leave Eindhoven. Only those who live here, or can commute from a nearby city, can be in the room.

There is a real request for something online. We can consider this for the future, working with the school so that it can offer it. We have also run this kind of programme before the graduation show. Then other things get in the way: a break, the show itself, too little time after exams. There is no perfect moment. Holding it after every other obligation has one clear cost: the graduates leave.

## Budgets that were too big

In Module 4 the teams budgeted a project together with Our project budget. They really discussed it collectively, and that was good to see. But the budgets came out very large, well over €150,000. As we said to each other afterwards, it is quite easy to imagine a budget that size, "but I don't think that necessarily makes it easier", or more interesting.

Participants told us they found the personal project budget of the second evening more useful than the team one. I read that as the same point. Gemma put it from her own practice: when she thinks about collaboration today, it is "not necessarily autonomy, but this interdependence": a local organisation that hosts the exhibition, a regional one that helps with the research, each doing their part.

So next time the team exercise gets grounded: a ceiling of around €50,000 to €60,000, near the upper end of what the Stimuleringsfonds gives, and a real partner in the scenario, such as a venue whose costs you know. Within a grounded scenario, the teams can still be free and imaginative.

## The editor's table

After the break we sat around one large table: the bookmarks with lines from the earlier modules, a printed transcript of the first conversation passed from hand to hand, highlighters, snacks and the recorders. The participants talked; I moderated, and Gemma and Ned pitched in.

The most interesting moments came when the conversation went beyond the specifics and the participants spoke about how they could do it in general: what now, after school. Gemma could pull in examples from the Arte Útil archive at the right moment; the table was full of cases to draw on. We will keep improving our moderation, so the conversation stays lively and on topic.

## Room for the conversation

These three evenings were intense. We built a tool for each step, and the steps followed each other: an hourly rate, then a quote that becomes an invoice, then a budget for a project of your own, then a budget for a project as a team. I believe that sequence mattered: each tool built on the one before.

The tools compressed time. The pen-and-paper work of calculating numbers and filling in forms was largely done for us. Instead, the participants needed to learn how to use the tools on a phone, but this was a group very comfortable with apps. We trusted the tools to do their job. Taking care of the mundane, they bought us the time, and the mind space, for discussion, for building teams and for the content itself. Working with Gemma, the choice to give much of that time to discussion, and to good examples from Arte Útil and Seeds of Hope, was the right call.

## A publication that adds up

The publication was not part of the school's assignment. It was ambitious, and I think it is a good ambition.

After the last evening we talked about what it could become. We agreed it could be "a guiding document for the next thing": each edition starts from what the previous group found. We want to build something that can add up: each edition adds to the last, a shared source of knowledge for future editions and generations, whichever school hosts it. That is the premise we will bring to the participants who join us in writing it. We give it a frame, a sandbox, and rely on their creativity to see what it becomes.

In a sense this is a Situated Studio's live practice of a knowledge commons. A graduate who couldn't join wrote to us, pleasantly surprised at how much of the workshop is already published and open to study, and surprised too that we share it to this extent. "Is that even okay?" they asked. I think the sharing itself is the demonstration. Commoning is something you practise, in the open.

## Write it with us

The first shared edition will be written with some of the participants this month, every one of them credited as an author. We meet in Eindhoven for two, at most three, writing sessions. If you took part and want to join, please get in touch.
