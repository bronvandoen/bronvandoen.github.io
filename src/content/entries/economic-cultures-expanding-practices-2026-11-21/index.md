On Saturday 21 November I took Economic Cultures to Expanding Practices, a day for starting creatives at the Nieuwe Instituut in Rotterdam. The trains were packed and a little late, but I made it in time for lunch, with a handful of familiar faces, Ned Kaar, the DAE Alumni Coordinator, among them. The Nieuwe Instituut is always a pleasure to visit: the café and the library, the exhibitions and the workspaces, in a creative part of the city. Our session, Budget your ambition, together, ran twice in the afternoon, between a panel and the drinks.

## Collective work, on stage

The panel before our session, Shifting Conditions of Practice, brought together Kurina Sohn, Frank Verkade, Dewi Kruijk, Liv Vaisberg and Nienke van der Wal, moderated by Jamil Fiorino-Habib. Several of them work in collectible design, which is not my field, and I expected to hear more about individual careers. Instead they kept coming back to working together: collective work, collective projects. My assumption was wrong, and I was glad of it.

It also explained to me why the Nieuwe Instituut made room for Economic Cultures. The practice of collective work is where the blind spots are, and that is where we work. Two of the panel joined our session afterwards, one in each round.

## Half an hour in Rotterdam

We worked in the Re-centre, a comfortable corner of the research space with sofas. Teams of three to five people, most of whom had just met, each picked a situation drawn from Rotterdam today: what the harbour remembers, an empty shop the city bought, a market of 300 stalls, the next hot summer, work that counts in Zuid. In half an hour, each team wrote a budget of about €55,000 for a cultural project: who does every hour, who pays every euro, within the rules of Fonds Lef, an imaginary fund for the creative industries.

It was tight. Several participants told me the exercise needs 45 minutes. It was not a complaint; they were pleased with what they had made in so little time. But if we have thirty minutes, we owe people a thirty-minute experience, and we will fit the next edition to its time.

What landed, for the participants and for the Nieuwe Instituut, was situated knowledge. Cultural innovation can be thought from a region and its present state. A project tied to its place can find support beyond the creative industries: from social work, from the municipality, even from business. The teams found their roles in a group, made sure none of their work was invisible in the budget, and placed every line with one of three sources: the main backer, a regional backer, and a third partner or the project's own earnings.

One thing we had no time for was the leading role. Our live report showed that in three of the four teams one person carried most of the leading hours. It had already come up in the panel: who carries the work that is not design, and how it stays invisible. Next time, we will make time for it.

The four plans, as the teams sent them that afternoon, are on [the live report](/entries/expanding-practices-live-report-2026-11-21/).

## A tender room

I felt it at Design Academy Eindhoven, and I felt it here: the people we work with are tender. They are inexperienced and truly curious, and there is a rawness to them, a slight hesitation in their position. It showed in what they asked the panel and in how they worked in the rounds. Some were very careful about even saying what they bring to the table.

At the drinks, a few of them told me plainly that they felt out of their element and didn't know who to talk to. I told them it is already something that they came. This is an occasion to walk up to someone you found interesting, in the panel, in a workshop, or someone you have heard of and never spoken to, and say hello. You don't need a pitch or a portfolio. Tell them how you found the event, and what you learned and missed; they want to hear it. The people here gave up a Saturday evening to meet new people and hear new perspectives. You don't need to be more professional than you already are.

## Economic Cultures counts pennies

Gemma and I often ask ourselves why we are still independent, and what we are doing in Eindhoven. The topics we care about can rarely be handled by small practices like ours alone. There are many institutions working on them from within, where we would often be better equipped, and almost always better paid, with more security. Bianca Elzenbaumer's thesis Designing Economic Cultures (Goldsmiths, 2013) describes how designers' engagement fades by around the age of 35, worn down by precarious work, and how many leave the profession between 35 and 40 (p. 38). We are both well past that age, still here, and most of the time we call it surviving.

Working inside is not easier. Institutions have more resources than any one of us, and their name brings trust, access and privileges. They also face thin budgets, little support for different perspectives, and rules they must keep to. When an institution brings in people like us, its own staff may rightly ask: what do they bring that I don't, and is my work in question?

Outside, Economic Cultures overlaps too. It touches business basics, bookkeeping, plan writing, portfolios, where to find help, and for each of those there are coaches, plan writers, foundations and other dedicated practices. Some of them were at Expanding Practices too, and they do their part better than we could.

So we never assume we know better. When we work with graduates, we trust their talent because we trust their school: a diploma is a seal of approval, earned by the students and by the teachers who taught them. We trust the entrepreneurial courses inside, and the practices outside.

What we never shy away from is counting pennies. In the creative industries, inside school and out, money is a topic people avoid, or think beneath them. For us it is daily. We weigh what a workshop like this one pays against what we think the work is worth, and whether any discount is worth taking. Right now we are working out how to fund a full Agents of Change project in Eindhoven. Whether you are one year out of school or ten, that weighing never stops, and it is what burns people out and drives them towards something steadier. Everything we teach in Economic Cultures is real for us, almost depressingly so.

## A first footing

That is why we call ourselves navigators. Economic Cultures is the first footing: a safe place to talk about money, to count every hour you need to protect, for your work and your life, and to place each one with someone who pays for it. Then we point you on: to your school, to the sister practices outside, and to the people in the room.

This is how we see a future with the Nieuwe Instituut: trusting what it and its partners already do, we help people find their footing, find each other, and find who to reach out to: people to practise their work with, and the right partners and audiences for their potential. We also demonstrated how cooperation can be tied to the region where people live and work, in this case how creative talent can be tied to Rotterdam. We look forward to taking this line of expanding practices further.

A few young creatives asked if they could join the full Economic Cultures programme. For Design Academy alumni I had a practical answer: Ned was in the room, and the next editions will actively invite alumni back. For everyone else, I could only say: keep an eye on the Nieuwe Instituut. Maybe we'll be back.
