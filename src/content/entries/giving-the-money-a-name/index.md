This morning Gemma and I went through the hourly-rate tool for Economic Cultures, step by step, on our own phones. It's for recent design graduates working out what an hour of their work needs to earn. At step seven, "versions of me", where you try out how your rate changes when your life does, Gemma told a story.

A friend of hers had a decent salary and still struggled with the cost of living. One day his grandfather, whose eyesight was going, asked him to come along to the bank. There he found out that his grandfather had opened an account for each grandchild. All his working life, on a modest fixed income, he had put a small amount into each one every month. Everything was written down. Now the money was there, waiting for them.

The lesson he took from his grandfather, as Gemma tells it: all the money you have, the money you pay and the money you move, needs to have a name.

Gemma's own father was an accountant. In the supermarket he still writes every item down on a piece of paper, and at the end he checks it against the receipt. She called it "that kind of culture of taking care of the coin." Money has become so liquid, she said, that we lose it.

It reminded me of the older generation in Korea, who would quietly build savings for a child. And it put into words why the versions page is there. It isn't a forecast. It's a place to give the money a name: a pet, a part-time job, moving in together, or a couple of hundred euros a month set aside for a reason that is only yours.

For people like us, money isn't the goal. But how you manage it says something about who you are and how you want to live. Up to step six, the tool counts everything we expect to carry, the good and the bad. Then it turns. Money stops being a black hole that disappears into the state, the landlord and the subscriptions. It becomes planning for things to blossom, not only for things to go wrong: for yourself, your family and, as Gemma added, the people you love.

Gemma put the effect simply: once you name the cost, the income, everything, you feel you own it, and that you can change it. I think it also makes you less apologetic about your rate. If every euro in it has a purpose, you can stand behind the number, however ambitious it looks.

We know the numbers can scare a graduate. Gemma wants each workshop day to have a small "piece of hope" for that moment. This story may be the first one.
