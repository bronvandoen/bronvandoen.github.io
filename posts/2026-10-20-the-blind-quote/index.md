---
title: The blind quote
date: 2026-10-20
summary: Why the quote-to-invoice exercise is a role play, and why a client who hides the budget is the one with the poor practice.
project: Economic Cultures
voices: Minsung Wang and Gemma Medina
---
"I really like your work." And then nothing about the budget. If you're starting out as an artist or designer, you've probably heard this. You're asked for a quote, you don't know what they have in mind, and you guess. I call it a blind quote. I've had to write them my whole career.

A freelance developer can say: my rate is 150 euros an hour, take it or leave it. For artists and designers that kind of openness isn't automatic. Established names get told the budget upfront. People starting out mostly don't, and the client often expects them to undershoot. Say there is a budget of 5,000 euros. They know you're not experienced, so they expect a quote of 2,000, or 1,500. And you're already shaking: is this too much, can I even ask this? I got agitated just explaining it to Gemma.

So I want to say it plainly to the graduates: it's not your fault. Hiding the budget is poor practice, and it's on the client. Sharing a budget doesn't mean you get all of it. It means you can write an honest quote.

That's why the quote-to-invoice exercise in Economic Cultures is a role play. Everyone takes a turn as the designer and as the client: a brief, a quote, a negotiation, a handshake, an invoice. In a workshop you can try both sides of the table safely, before it counts. The Fair Practice Code runs through it, not as a lecture (taught as a concept, it rarely sticks with students) but at the moments it comes alive: agree before you start, name what isn't included, get the yes in writing.

Playing the client teaches something too. Fair practice is for everyone. The day you give an assignment yourself, you can be the unfair one: leaning on unpaid interns, or making demands because of your position or your reputation.

Gemma brought in what I hoped she would. How you get paid depends on the field. As a researcher you're often paid by the hour, and the conversation is about what you need, the deadlines, and being clear about what's included. Curatorial work is mostly a fixed sum: this is the money we have, can you make an exhibition? And paying artists fairly is quite recent. Before, she said, a lot of artists exhibited without getting a fee. In the Netherlands, a guideline for artists' fees at exhibitions has only existed since 2017.

That fixed sum is exactly why your hourly rate still matters. Nobody asks for your hours. It's your job to know what, say, 3,000 euros can honestly deliver, and then to negotiate: talk the ambition down, or the fee up. And when a client says "we like your work", they've already admitted its value. You don't need to be ashamed to stand up for its price.

Gemma named what the role play is for. Nobody talks about this, so nobody knows how to navigate it. Here they practise not being ashamed to ask for what they need.
